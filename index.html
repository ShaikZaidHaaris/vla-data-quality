Step 01 · the benchmark

One task, three data sources, matched budgets

Everything except the data is held still.

The task is robosuite Lift. A Franka Panda picks a cube off a table. RoboMimic recorded that same task three times, and the only thing that changed was who did the recording: one skilled teleoperator (PH), six operators of varying skill (MH), and an RL agent left to run (MG).

Budgets are matched on frames, not episodes. MH and MG episodes run much longer, so counting episodes would have quietly given them more data and turned this into a study of volume.

Step 02 · the result

Expert beats mixed beats machine

Identical model, 1,000 steps each, evaluated on 100 fresh scenes never used to select a checkpoint.

This is the ordering RoboMimic already published, so the benchmark recovers a result the field agrees on before being asked anything new. MG is the outlier. Zero successes and zero grasps in a hundred attempts puts it level with random flailing.

Why MG collapsed

Only 26.7% of MG's demonstrations actually finish the task. The rest run out the clock and fail, so the policy learned to fail too. Its training loss says the same thing: 1.413, against roughly 1.09 for the other two.

Step 03 · diagnosis

Where the best policy actually breaks

Knowing PH wins is not actionable. What stops it from winning by more?

Every rollout passes through reach, grasp, lift and place. Scoring each step separately turns one number into a map of where policies die. The map checks out: multiplying the four probabilities reproduces the observed success rate exactly, for all three datasets.

Fixing grasp alone would take overall success from 0.150 to 0.564, a gain of 41.4 points. The next best fix is worth 6.9.

What goes wrong is physical. Failures stall 4.7 cm short of the cube; successes get to 1.6 cm. The behaviour that separates them most is how directly the arm travels, where every standard deviation multiplies the odds of success by 5.4.

Step 04 · hardening

The obvious fix would have failed

Grasping is the bottleneck, so the obvious move is to go collect grasping data. Comparing the three sources shows that would have been wasted effort.

A weakness that shows up in every dataset cannot belong to any one of them. Grasp succeeds 25% of the time on expert data, 14% on mixed, never on machine, and that spread does not separate statistically (p = 0.73). Even the corpus where every single demonstration succeeds fails at this step. The limit is in the training recipe, not the data.

Reaching behaves the opposite way. It runs from 0.97 down to 0.20, the gap is clearly separated, and expert data already demonstrates that 0.97 is reachable. That gives you something concrete to aim at.

Step 05 · the recipe

Six points, no training

If the recipe is what limits grasping, changing the recipe should move it. That can be tested at inference time, on the model already trained.

The policy predicts a chunk of actions, then executes several of them before looking again. Running eight overshoots during the last few centimetres. Running four keeps the gripper on target as it closes.

Changing one setting bought +5.9 points, and +9 points of grasp rate, with no retraining at all. It moved exactly the step that had been ruled out as a data problem.

Step 06 · the test

Does following the prescription work?

The claim is causal: select data by path efficiency and the policy improves.

Both arms take the same trained model and train it 50 steps further. One adds the 107 most direct demonstrations from MH. The other adds 98 picked at random. Same checkpoint, same steps, same learning rate, same seed, same evaluation scenes, and frame budgets that match to 0.03%. The only thing that differs is which demonstrations were chosen.

Also measured

Where the cube starts does not predict success (−0.021 at full sample size). Generic grasping data will not help, since every corpus fails there. Curating down to a subset of data already in hand scored 37.58, below the 40.71 baseline, because throwing out weaker demonstrations also throws out information. Train for 500 steps rather than 1,000 (42.80 falls to 29.37 by then), and execute four actions per inference rather than eight.
